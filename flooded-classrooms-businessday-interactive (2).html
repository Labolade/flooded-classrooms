Flooded classrooms are shutting Nigerian children out of school

Repeated floods have damaged schools, displaced children and cut off routes to classrooms across Nigeria. BusinessDay tracked 91 schools with documented flood impacts and found that some communities have faced repeated disruption for years.

FAWAZ ADEBISI & BECKY OJEH · INVESTIGATION

For the rest of Mallam Hassan Umar’s life, May 29, 2025, will remain a day he never wishes to remember. While President Bola Tinubu was marking his second anniversary as Nigeria’s president, Umar was mourning the loss of four young boys to a catastrophic flash flood in Tiffin Maza, an agrarian community in Mokwa, Niger State.

A high-velocity wall of water, rising as high as seven feet, tore through the community, killing more than 200 people, leaving hundreds missing and destroying homes and schools in its path.

“The school walls had been washed away and the roof torn apart.”

Umar was not alone. Another teacher, Haruna Yusuf, lost 12 Almajiri pupils taught by his father, bringing the number of students killed in the two schools to 16.

The disruption is bigger than the classroom

Flooding can destroy a school, turn it into a shelter, block the road to it or force families to withdraw children altogether.

Collapsed or damaged classrooms can make schools unusable even after floodwater recedes.

Where the risk is concentrated

NiHSA’s September 2026 assessment identified more than 4,500 schools at risk across 15 states. BusinessDay’s documented cases show the problem on the ground.

A problem that keeps returning

Several schools appeared repeatedly in records from different years, suggesting that flooding is not always a one-off disruption.

2010

Flooding in Ajegunle forced the relocation of the community’s only public secondary school about four kilometres away.

2019

Flooding prevented about 650 pupils from accessing Ezigbo Primary School, Amansea.

2022

Community Secondary School, Abacheke, Imo State, became inaccessible after flooding, with teachers leaving and parents withdrawing or moving children to schools outside the community.

2024

Floods affected 115 schools in Borno, Adamawa and Yobe; 39 were destroyed and 76 became shelters.

2026

Flooding and erosion destroyed two classroom blocks at Ezi-Awka Community Secondary School, while flooding also disrupted schools in Rivers and other states.

What happens when the water recedes?

Mojeed Alabi, an education journalist and president of the Education Writers Association of Nigeria, said authorities should consider relocating schools repeatedly affected by flooding rather than allowing pupils and teachers to return to unsafe facilities each year.

“It affects the performance, it affects retention, it affects participation, and at the end of the day, it affects the whole education system in the country.”
Methodology

BusinessDay analysed Nigeria Hydrological Services Agency advisories alongside government alerts, media reports, academic studies and field accounts, identifying 91 schools with documented flood impacts. Thirty-five had stronger evidence, including official acknowledgement, visual documentation, structural damage or repeated flooding.

What the data does not show

The investigation does not establish that every naira of the ₦623.34bn received by the 26 LGAs was available for flood prevention or school rehabilitation. It also does not treat the 4,500+ schools identified by NiHSA as schools already flooded; they were identified as being at risk.
